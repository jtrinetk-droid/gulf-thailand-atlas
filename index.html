Every night, hundreds of gas platforms light up the Gulf of Thailand. Next to them sits a wedge of sea the size of Rwanda that has stayed dark for fifty years. This is the story of that wedge, and why it now matters more than the Strait of Hormuz for Thailand's energy security.

01

The dispute in sixty seconds

No energy or legal background needed. Three things to understand before the map.

Two countries drew two different lines

Countries own the seabed off their coasts, but where two coasts face each other someone has to draw the dividing line. In 1972 Cambodia drew one that runs straight west through the middle of Thailand's island of Koh Kut, then turns south. In 1973 Thailand drew its own line, giving Koh Kut full weight, which pushes the line far to the east. The sea between the two lines is the Overlapping Claims Area, or OCA.

Thailand runs on Gulf gas, and the Gulf is running down

Gas makes more than half of Thailand's electricity, and most of that gas has come from the Gulf of Thailand since 1981. Those fields are now mature: Erawan, the anchor field, produced around 1,200 million cubic feet a day in 2020 and about 800 today. The shortfall is covered by liquefied natural gas (LNG) shipped in by sea, roughly a quarter of it through the Strait of Hormuz.

A deal existed. The 2025 war killed it

In 2001 the two governments signed a memorandum (MOU 2544, or "MOU 44") to negotiate the boundary and a joint gas scheme together. It produced almost nothing. In 2025 a border war and a political crisis in Bangkok made the whole subject toxic, and in May 2026 Thailand tore the MOU up. Cambodia took the dispute to a UN conciliation process whose advice is not binding.

Words you will see

OCA
Overlapping Claims Area: the roughly 26,000–27,000 km² of the Gulf claimed by both Thailand and Cambodia.
MOU 2544 / MOU 44
The 2001 memorandum of understanding (2544 in the Thai calendar). It split the OCA at latitude 11°N: the northern part to be divided, the southern part to be jointly developed, both negotiated together.
tcf
Trillion cubic feet of gas. Thailand uses about 1.7 tcf a year.
MMscfd
Million standard cubic feet per day, the unit used for field output. Thailand consumes roughly 4,500–4,700 MMscfd.
LNG
Liquefied natural gas, cooled and shipped by tanker. Priced on Asian spot markets, so a Middle East war raises Thai power bills within weeks.
Joint development
Both countries set the boundary question aside, create a shared authority, and split the oil and gas revenue. Thailand already does this with Malaysia.
UNCLOS conciliation
A procedure under the UN Law of the Sea Convention. A five-member commission hears both sides and writes a report within about a year. The report is advice, not a ruling.
Koh Kut (Ko Kood)
A 105 km² Thai island in Trat province, 2,600 residents. Cambodia's 1972 line crosses it, which is why the dispute touches Thai sovereignty and not just seabed rights.

03

A sea running out of gas

Why a seabed boundary is an energy-security problem, in four charts.

So what is in the OCA?

Nobody knows precisely, because nobody has drilled. Estimates circulating in 2024–2026 range from 11 trillion cubic feet of gas to 12 tcf plus 700 million barrels of oil; Thai press reports cite lower oil figures. Taken at face value, 11–12 tcf is six to seven years of Thailand's entire gas consumption, or roughly another Erawan running for a quarter-century. Even if the real number is half that, it is the largest undeveloped resource within reach of Thailand's existing pipelines, which pass within about 60–100 km of the wedge.

How we got here

A century of lines, deals and missed chances. Filter by theme.

    05

    Why the deal died in 2025

    A border war, a leaked phone call and a casino bill. Separate what is documented from what is inferred.

    The Paetongtarn Shinawatra government that took office in 2024 was the first in a decade with a working channel to Phnom Penh, through her father Thaksin's long personal alliance with Hun Sen. In November 2024 she said MOU 2544 could not be scrapped unilaterally and that talks would resume. Nationalist groups mobilised almost immediately around Koh Kut.

    Then the relationship broke. A clash at Ta Moan Thom on 28 May 2025 killed a Cambodian soldier. On 15 June Paetongtarn phoned Hun Sen to calm things; on 18 June he leaked the recording, in which she called him "uncle" and disparaged a Thai army commander. Her coalition partner walked out the same day. The Constitutional Court suspended her on 1 July and removed her on 29 August. In between, late July brought the heaviest fighting in a decade and the displacement of several hundred thousand people. The incoming government froze OCA energy talks in September "until the issue of the national border is resolved", campaigned on scrapping the MOU, and did so on 5 May 2026, two months into the Hormuz crisis.

    Documented

    • The sequence of events above, with dates, is well reported (TIME, Wikipedia timeline, The Nation).
    • A Hun Sen adviser tried in 2024 to buy about 20% of Bangchak, a Thai energy company; the Social Security Fund refused to sell. Gregory Raymond (ISEAS) reads this as an attempt to push Bangkok toward a joint-development treaty by private means.
    • Thailand's Entertainment Complex Bill, which would have legalised casinos, was in parliament during the crisis and was withdrawn on 7 July 2025, six days after the suspension. Cambodia's border casinos in Poipet are a significant revenue stream.
    • The OCA energy track was formally frozen on 9 September 2025 by energy minister Auttapol Rerkpiboon.

    Inferred, and disputed

    • That Hun Sen engineered Paetongtarn's fall over the OCA. No source states this as fact, and Raymond says it "cannot be ascertained conclusively". The strongest objection is internal: removing Paetongtarn destroyed the one Thai government likely to sign a gas deal. If the gas was the goal, the war was self-defeating.
    • That the casino bill was a cause. The timing is suggestive, but casino legalisation had been discussed in Bangkok for years without friction.
    • My reading. The OCA was the prize the Thaksin–Hun Sen relationship had failed to deliver, the casino bill was a threat to Cambodian revenue, and the leaked call was punishment for both. Thitinan Pongsudhirak's verdict, "it got personal", fits the evidence better than a plan. Either way the energy outcome is identical: the deal died with the government, and the dispute hardened just as Hormuz showed why the gas matters.

    The JDA took a generation to produce gas, which is a warning as much as a model. But its design did the hard thing: it let two governments make money from a seabed neither would concede. The one feature it lacked, and the OCA needs, is a way to take an island off the table.

    08

    What I would do

    Six moves for a Thai policymaker, in the order I would make them.

    1. Take Koh Kut off the table, in writing

      Thailand's position that there is "no dispute" over the island is correct and widely accepted. Say so jointly. A short statement that any future arrangement is without prejudice to Thai sovereignty over Koh Kut, signed by both governments inside the conciliation process, removes the only part of this dispute that moves Thai voters. Without it, no Thai official will sign anything, because Article 157 of the Constitution exposes them to prosecution for "political" decisions.

    2. Let the commission produce the baseline

      The UNCLOS conciliation report is due around mid-2027 and is not binding. That is a feature. An equidistance-based line proposed by a neutral commission is something neither government has to author or defend at home. Thailand should say now that it will treat the report as the technical starting point for talks, and spend the intervening months making sure the commission has Thailand's data.

    3. Re-separate the two tracks

      MOU 2544 tied joint development in the south to delimitation in the north, so neither moved. Replace it with two instruments: a joint-development agreement for the southern zone on the Malaysia–Thailand template, with a Joint Authority, a 50:50 split and a fixed term; and a delimitation track for the north that continues in parallel, with a review clause if it is not settled within, say, ten years.

    4. Start with a joint survey, not a treaty

      The biggest uncertainty is geological: the 11–12 tcf figures rest on 1970s seismic. A jointly funded, commission-supervised seismic survey is cheap, fits the confidence-building measures both sides agreed in October 2026, and produces the first new fact in this dispute in fifty years. If the resource is smaller than claimed, better to learn that before spending political capital.

    5. Ring-fence the revenue for resilience

      Thailand's Hormuz lesson in 2026 was fiscal: the Oil Fund spent about 99 billion baht hiding the price of fuel. Thailand's share of any OCA revenue should go by law into two things the crisis showed it lacks: a state-owned strategic petroleum reserve, and grid and storage investment for the renewables build-out in PDP 2026. Gas revenue that funds the exit from gas is the only version of this project that survives a net-zero argument.

    6. Make the energy case publicly, and make it about price

      Nationalism won the argument in 2024–2026 because the other side of the ledger was never shown. The government should publish what the OCA stalemate costs: the LNG cargoes bought at Singapore spot prices, the Oil Fund deficit, the power tariff deferrals. The gas in the wedge is cheaper than the next war in the Gulf. That is an argument that nationalism cannot answer, and nobody has made it.

    And the view from Phnom Penh

    Cambodia produces almost no oil or gas of its own and imports all its fuel, which is why Thailand's 2026 export ban closed a third of its petrol stations within weeks. Phnom Penh's interest in a deal is therefore at least as strong as Bangkok's, but its leverage is different: it wants the boundary settled on equidistance terms that reduce Koh Kut's effect, and it has moved the dispute into an international forum where that argument gets a hearing. A Thai proposal that offers money and certainty in the south in exchange for an explicit island carve-out in the north is one Cambodia can accept without losing face, because it is the same trade Malaysia made in 1979.

    09

    What could go wrong

    The honest list. Each of these could sink the plan.

    The gas may not be there

    Every figure in this atlas for the OCA's resources is an estimate from seismic work done before 1973. Fields in the adjacent Pattani basin are small and compartmentalised, which is why Thailand needs hundreds of platforms. The OCA could be the same, and smaller fields mean higher costs.

    It arrives too late to matter

    Even a deal signed in 2027 means first gas around 2035–2037. By then PDP 2026 aims for 65% or more clean power and Thai gas demand is projected to peak. The gas could be a bridge the country has already crossed.

    Stranded assets and the climate case

    New gas infrastructure built in the late 2030s risks competing with cheaper solar and storage. The case for the OCA is as a replacement for imported LNG, not as new demand; if it becomes a reason to build more gas plants, it is a mistake.

    Thai domestic politics

    The 2024 Koh Kut campaign and the 2009 attempt to cancel the MOU show how quickly this becomes a sovereignty fight. A conciliation report that gives Koh Kut less than full effect would be read in Bangkok as a loss, whatever the gas.

    Cambodian politics

    Decision-making in Phnom Penh runs through the Hun family and a patronage network for which border casinos and scam compounds are revenue. A deal that cuts across those interests, or that Hun Sen cannot claim as a win, may not hold.

    The money is not only Thailand's

    A 50:50 split means Thailand gives up half of what it claims as fully its own. The counter-argument is arithmetic: half of a producing field is worth more than all of a field that never gets drilled, which is what the last 54 years have delivered.

    Method & sources

    How the maps were built, what is approximate, and where every number comes from.

    Geometry

    Neither government publishes an official digital boundary of its claim, so the two lines on the map are reconstructions. Three anchors are documented: the 1907 treaty land-boundary point at 11°38.8′N 102°54.3′E (listed in Cambodia's 1982 maritime decree, UN DOALOS), Koh Kut's position (Natural Earth), and Point K of the 1997 Thailand–Vietnam boundary at 8°46′54.8″N 102°12′11.7″E (Sovereign Limits, IBRU), which lies on Cambodia's southern claim limit. Cambodia's line is drawn west from the treaty point through Koh Kut and then south to Point K, following published descriptions that it "passes through Koh Kut, continues into the Gulf and turns south". Thailand's line runs from the treaty point to the midpoint between Koh Kut and Cambodia's Koh Kong island, then along a median line to Point K. The westward extent of Cambodia's line was then calibrated so that the areas north and south of 11°N match the published MOU 2544 figures of about 10,000 and 16,000 km². The result is about 27,400 km², inside the 26,000–27,000 km² range quoted by Thai and international sources. Expect errors of a few kilometres along each line; the shape and position are reliable, the exact vertices are not.

    The Malaysia–Thailand JDA polygon uses the seven turning points published in the 1979 MOU. Coastlines are Natural Earth 10m. Field markers are field centres from MarineLink's offshore register where available (marked "verified" in the popup) and operator descriptions otherwise (marked "approximate"). Pipelines are schematic. Power plants are gas-fired plants of 300 MW or more from the WRI Global Power Plant Database.

    Imagery

    Satellite base: Esri World Imagery. Night lights: NASA Earth Observations / GIBS, VIIRS "Earth at Night" composite and the daily VIIRS Day/Night Band, served live from NASA. The bright cluster in the central Gulf is gas flaring and platform lighting; it is the most direct satellite evidence of where Thailand's production is, and of where it is not.

    Derived numbers

    LNG imports for 2021 are estimated from S&P Global's reported monthly figures (0.52 Mt/month January–September, 0.8 Mt in October) and 2022 from the reported 40% rise to 11.55 Mt in 2023. The 2024 supply mix converts 11.3 Mt of LNG to about 1,500 MMscfd, applies Kasikorn's 72:28 LNG-to-Myanmar import split, and uses S&P's 2.64 Bcf/d domestic figure. "Six to seven years of demand" divides 11–12 tcf by Thai consumption of about 1.7 tcf a year. Path ratings in chapter 07 are the author's judgement.
